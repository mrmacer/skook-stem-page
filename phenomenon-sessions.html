IU29 STEM Ecosystem — Professional Development

What if teachers were students again?

Phenomenon Sessions place educators inside genuine intellectual uncertainty — not simulated, not scripted — in direct contact with the real professionals who live inside the problems we want students to explore.

This is not a field trip. This is not a guest speaker. This is a structured experience designed to produce the cognitive state that precedes real learning — and to make that state visible to the teacher experiencing it.

The core problem we're solving

STEELS and 3D science ask teachers to facilitate phenomena-driven learning — but most teachers have never experienced it as a learner. We ask them to teach through uncertainty they have never inhabited. The result is compliance without comprehension.

"You cannot teach someone to swim by explaining buoyancy. At some point, they have to get in the water."

Phenomenon Sessions put teachers in the water. The phenomenon is real. The expert is real. The discomfort is real. The debrief names what just happened — and maps it directly to the classroom experience we're asking teachers to create.

What makes this different

01

Real problems

Not pedagogical simulations. Actual challenges from industry, manufacturing, and community partners who face them daily.

02

Real experts

Not curriculum consultants. Engineers, conservation ecologists, fabricators, infrastructure planners — people who don't have a lesson plan.

03

Real uncertainty

Problems are selected specifically because they may not have a clean answer. The point is the process, not the solution.

04

Named metacognition

Every session closes with explicit connection back to STEELS practices — labeled in the moment, not in a handout three weeks later.

The STEELS connection

Phenomenon Sessions are not adjacent to 3D science — they ARE 3D science, experienced from the learner's seat. Each session activates all three dimensions simultaneously:

Science & Engineering Practices Crosscutting Concepts Disciplinary Core Ideas
What happens in the session STEELS practice activated What teachers feel
Expert presents a real operational problem Asking questions & defining problems Confusion. "I don't know where to start."
Groups build initial explanations with limited data Constructing explanations & designing solutions Frustration. "We need more information."
Models are built, revised, argued over Developing & using models Engagement. "Oh — wait, what if..."
Groups share out — expert responds Obtaining, evaluating & communicating information Surprise. "We were wrong. And that was useful."
Facilitator names the experience Metacognitive reflection Recognition. "My students feel like this every day."

Example phenomena by sector

Manufacturing / industry

  • How does a fabricator decide which material fails last when everything is under pressure?
  • Why do production lines that look identical produce different defect rates?
  • What data would you need to predict machine failure before it happens?

Community / environment

  • How does an overpass change the hydrology of the watershed beneath it?
  • What does 100 years of ecological succession tell us about human disruption?
  • How does a municipality plan infrastructure when population models disagree?

The goal is not for teachers to become experts in the phenomenon. The goal is for teachers to remember what it feels like not to know — and to build classrooms that honor that feeling rather than eliminate it.

Facilitator Guide — Internal Use

Running a Phenomenon Session

This guide walks you through the full arc of a Phenomenon Session — from expert recruitment to debrief close. Do not skip the metacognitive frame. That's the whole point.

3–4
hours, full session
8–24
participants ideal
1
expert lead (non-educator)

Before you start: expert prep

Your expert is the most important variable. They need one briefing call (30 min) before the session. The goal: make sure they bring a real problem, not a presentation about their job.

Tell your expert exactly this:

"Don't explain what you do. Bring us a problem you actually haven't fully solved — or one where the answer surprised you when you found it. We'll work on it together. You're not the teacher. You're the expert in the room."

Common expert failure modes to coach around:

  • Showing a PowerPoint about their company instead of presenting a problem
  • Having a "right answer" ready to reveal at the end like a magic trick
  • Simplifying the problem to make it feel accessible — resist this
  • Answering questions immediately instead of redirecting to the group

Session arc

1
Phase one
Phenomenon launch
20–30 min

Expert presents the phenomenon — ideally with artifacts, data, images, or physical samples. No framing of "what we're going to learn today." Just: here's a situation. What do you notice? What do you wonder?

Facilitator captures notice/wonder on a shared surface (whiteboard, miro, chart paper). Do not sort or curate yet.

SEP: asking questions CCC: patterns
2
Phase two
Initial model building
25–35 min

Groups of 3–4 construct an initial explanation — drawn, diagrammed, written, or built. The constraint: they must commit to a claim before receiving additional data. This is the discomfort zone. Don't rescue them.

Facilitator circulates. Listen for: "we need more information" (good), "I don't understand the problem" (redirect back to the artifact), "what are we supposed to do here?" (normal — don't answer it).

SEP: developing models SEP: constructing explanations CCC: cause & effect
3
Phase three
Data injection + revision
20–25 min

Expert provides one additional piece of information — a data set, a constraint, a variable they didn't share. Groups revise their models. The key: they must show what changed and why.

This is where real thinking happens. Watch for groups that don't change anything — that's a discussion point in debrief.

SEP: analyzing & interpreting data CCC: systems thinking
4
Phase four
Gallery share + expert response
25–30 min

Groups post models. Gallery walk — everyone leaves sticky notes on other groups' models (questions, not critiques). Then expert responds to each model, not to "correct" but to engage. Where were they close? Where did they miss something he/she didn't expect them to miss?

SEP: obtaining & communicating information SEP: engaging in argument from evidence
5
Phase five — do not skip
The metacognitive frame
30–40 min

Facilitator leads the explicit debrief. The sequence matters:

  • What did you feel at each phase? (Confusion, frustration, curiosity, surprise) — name these
  • When your students are in your classroom, which of these feelings are you creating? Which are you eliminating?
  • What would you have to change about your practice to create the conditions that produced what you felt today?
  • Name the STEELS practices you enacted — without having been told to enact them

Close with: "This is what 3D science feels like from inside it. Your job is to build this for your students, without telling them what it's called."

Common facilitation mistakes

MistakeWhy it kills the sessionThe fix
Rescuing teachers when they're frustrated Eliminates the productive struggle — which is the lesson Say: "Sit with that. What's another way to look at it?"
Letting the expert lecture Turns it back into a presentation Interrupt early: "Can you hold that? Let's have the group respond first."
Skipping or rushing the metacognitive debrief Teachers walk out with an experience but no frame for applying it Block 30–40 min. This is non-negotiable.
Choosing a phenomenon with a clean answer Teachers feel like they "got it" and the vulnerability disappears Vetting question: "Would your team argue about this?" If no — pick a different problem.

For District Administrators

One day. Real change.

Phenomenon Sessions give your teachers something most PD never does: the experience of being a learner inside the kind of thinking STEELS actually requires.

You have teachers who understand STEELS as a compliance document. Phenomenon Sessions are how you get teachers who understand STEELS as a practice.

The honest problem with most science PD

Teachers leave workshops with frameworks, vocabulary, and annotated standards documents. They return to classrooms and do what they've always done — because the PD never changed what they believe learning feels like.

STEELS and 3D science require teachers to facilitate uncertainty. Most PD eliminates uncertainty. That's the gap.

Traditional PD

  • Expert presents content
  • Teachers take notes
  • Activity demonstrates a concept
  • Handout connects to standards
  • Teachers leave with materials

Phenomenon Sessions

  • Industry expert presents a real problem
  • Teachers build models and argue
  • Confusion is intentional and named
  • STEELS practices are enacted, then identified
  • Teachers leave with a new frame for their practice

What you're actually buying

One day of release time

Per teacher, per session. We handle scheduling coordination and can work within existing Act 80 or flex PD days.

Industry relationships

IU29 sources and prepares all expert partners. Your district gains a relationship — not just a one-time visit.

STEELS documentation

Each session produces evidence of SEP engagement that maps directly to your Act 48 and STEELS implementation records.

The single question worth asking your teachers after this session: "When was the last time you didn't know the answer — and had to work toward it anyway?" If they can't remember, that's the problem. Phenomenon Sessions fix it.

For Industry & Community Partners

Your problem. Their classroom.

We're not asking you to explain what you do. We're asking you to bring a problem you actually live with — and let teachers work on it alongside you.

You don't need a lesson plan. You don't need a curriculum background. You need a problem that's real, a willingness to say "I don't know" when you don't know, and about half a day.

Why this matters to you

↑

Workforce pipeline

Teachers who understand your industry's real problems design learning that points students toward your field — not away from it.

→

Community visibility

You become a named partner in IU29's regional STEM ecosystem — connected to 14 school communities across Schuylkill County: 12 public school districts and two non-public partner schools.

◎

Fresh perspective

Sometimes the best insight into a stuck problem comes from people who've never seen it before. Teachers ask questions your team stopped asking years ago.

What we ask of you

1
Before the session
One 30-minute prep call

We'll help you shape your problem into a phenomenon — concrete, real, arguable. We do the pedagogical design. You just bring the expertise.

2
The session
Half day on-site or at your facility

Present your problem. Watch teachers work on it. Respond to their models. No slides required. No script. Just expertise in a room with people who genuinely want to understand what you do.

3
After the session
Optional continued engagement

Some partners become recurring contributors. Some connect directly with specific teachers or programs. Some just send us their next interesting problem. Any level of continued involvement is welcome.

What makes a good Phenomenon Session problem?

Good fit

  • A real operational challenge without a clean solution
  • Something your team has genuinely argued about
  • A decision that required trade-offs between competing factors
  • Something that surprised you when you found the answer — or still hasn't resolved

Not a good fit

  • A presentation about your company history or products
  • A problem with a single correct answer you'll reveal at the end
  • Anything that requires weeks of domain knowledge to engage with
  • A tour without a challenge attached

Who's already at the table

Phenomenon Sessions connect to IU29's broader STEM ecosystem — 12 public school districts, two non-public partner schools, and established relationships with workforce development, conservation organizations, manufacturing, and infrastructure sectors across Schuylkill County. Your participation puts you inside a regional network, not a one-off event.

If you've ever thought "I wish more people understood what we actually do" — this is the most direct path to fixing that, one teacher cohort at a time.
